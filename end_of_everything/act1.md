# Act I: The Tectonic Clock

The basalt did not like to break. It had to be coaxed.

Eran stood waist-deep in the cold, churning green of the river, his left hand pressed flat against the submerged flank of the fifth pillar. Through his palm, he felt the vibration—not the surface rush of the water, but the deep, sub-audible hum running through the bedrock. It was a three-cycle pulse, steady as a heartbeat, radiating up from the mantle through the carved hexagonal columns.

Across the channel, his father, Varos, stood atop the upper terrace, framing the evening star through the notch of the sight-stone.

"Three arc-seconds off, Eran," Varos called down over the roar of the spillway. His voice was flat, carrying the dry, methodical precision of a man who viewed the sky as an equation that refused to balance. "The alignment is dragging east."

"It’s not the sky, Dad," Eran wiped river-foam from his eyes, his left forearm burning from the weight of the iron-shod lever. "The stone is binding. The ground beneath the footings is soft."

Varos didn't look down. He adjusted the brass-and-hardwood sighting arm on its pivot, checking the plumb line. "The footings are set sixty feet into solid basalt. The ground doesn't move."

"It moved this morning." Eran hauled himself up onto the timber scaffolding, his calloused skin dripping. He pointed toward the northern horizon, where the jagged teeth of the basalt canyon met the darkening sky. "The silt in the lower intake isn't river sand. It’s white. It’s glacial flour from the ice walls up-country."

Varos finally paused, his hand resting on the polished sighting arm. For a fraction of a second, his posture broke—just enough for Eran to see the exhaustion etched into his father's shoulders. They had been adjusting the array for forty days, constantly recalculating as the magnetic drift pulled the sight-lines out of phase.

"The ice walls have stood for ten thousand years, Eran," Varos said quietly, turning to face him. "They are four hundred cubits thick."

"Then why is the water freezing my toes in the middle of summer?" Eran reached into his belt pouch and pulled out a small, hexagonal block of dark cedar—a hand-carved scale model of the fifth pillar, complete with miniature rocker grooves and counterweight notches. He set it on the flat stone between them. "If the river rises another two cubits, the hydraulic load on the gate-locks will exceed the tensile limit of the hardwood keys. The rollers won't hold."

Varos looked at the tiny wooden block in his son's hands. He didn't correct the math. He couldn't.

From deep up the canyon—fifty miles north, far beyond the stone plazas and the terraced orchards—came a sound that wasn't thunder. It was too low for thunder. It was the sound of a mountain snapping in half, muffled by distance, followed by a sudden, unnatural silence as the river in front of them dropped six inches in three seconds.

Varos looked down at the empty water-line on the basalt piers, then up at his son.

"Get your sister," Varos said.
